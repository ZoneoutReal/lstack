# Brief — Oversee, don't block: close the loop on work you can check

## Reader and promise
Operators and team leads, technical or not, who spend the day approving each agent step instead of checking results. Promise: a short test that separates precision work (a checkable, verifiable result an agent can iterate toward) from taste work (judgment, voice, preference, or a high-stakes call), plus a numbered oversight pattern they can run this week. The pattern puts clear goals and measured results inside the loop, so an overseer knows when to step in without approving every step, and so the loop can improve itself over time.

## Counterpoints
- Taste-heavy and high-stakes work stays in the loop, and someone has to own the misses. Airbnb’s guest behavior and Skift/McKinsey’s research-versus-booking split show that people still want to make preference-heavy choices themselves (claims 10 and 11). Crosby insured its agents and still kept lawyers on clearly legal work; the insurance answers who pays when a gray-area task goes wrong, not whether high-stakes legal work can run unsupervised (claim 12). Article 14 requires a named person who can override or stop a high-risk system (claim 9). Ownership is a person or a policy that pays — not “the agent.”
- A measure you optimize can stop measuring what you care about. Marilyn Strathern, restating Goodhart, wrote: “When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure” ([Strathern 1997, *European Review*](https://gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/1997-strathern.pdf)). DeepSeek reports reward hacking when a learned preference model, rather than a hard verifier, is the score (claim 3). Google’s OKR guide warns that OKRs are not a to-do list and not a stand-in for evaluating a person (claim 4). If the only check is “did the metric move,” an agent can hit the number and miss the point.
- Taking people out of routine work can leave them unready for the hard cases. Lisanne Bainbridge’s “Ironies of automation” (1983): after automation, the person is left to monitor the system and to take over if it fails; unused control skills deteriorate, and takeover usually happens when something is already wrong, so more skill is needed, not less ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-1098(83)90046-8); [open PDF](https://ckrybus.com/static/papers/Bainbridge_1983_Automatica.pdf)). Article 14’s warning about automation bias is the same problem in modern language (claim 9). An overseer who only watches a dashboard still needs enough practice to intervene well.
- “Checkable” is not the same as “no human needed.” Wei notes that some tasks are harder to verify than to produce (claim 2). Article 14 still requires effective oversight for high-risk systems even when you can measure them (claim 9). Incomplete checks (tests that miss a bug, a key result that does not cover the real objective) leave misses that the loop will not catch unless a person owns them.

## Examples / data
Use these as the numbered oversight pattern. The figures above are evidence that each step matters, not scores a reader should expect to copy.

1. **Sort the work.** Ask: can I write a check that most people would grade the same way? If yes — a total that matches, a test suite that passes, a form that is complete, a refund under a stated limit — it is precision work (claims 2 and 3). If good depends on voice, taste, or a judgment call people would debate, it is taste work (claims 2, 10 and 11). If it goes to a client, moves money, makes a legal commitment, or cannot be undone, treat it as high-stakes even if some part of it is checkable (claims 1, 9 and 12).

2. **Write the goal and the measure before you step back.** Grove’s two questions: where do I want to go, and how will I know I am getting there (claim 4)? A key result names an outcome and the evidence you will accept, not a list of steps to approve. If you cannot write a key result that people would score the same way, you do not yet have a closed loop — stay in it.

3. **Give the agent the check, not a pile of step approvals.** For precision work, let the agent iterate until the check passes (claims 2 and 3). Approving every intermediate step makes the person the bottleneck (claim 1).

4. **Oversee by exception, using the measure.** Set the threshold for when a person steps in, the way an error budget tells a team to keep shipping or to halt and fix (claim 6), or the way NIST’s Measure then Manage path tells you to keep going or to override and stop (claims 7 and 9). This is how metrics let an overseer intervene without approving every step.

5. **Keep taste and high-stakes work in the loop.** Review before it goes out (claims 1, 10, 11 and 12). Help with research or a first draft is not the same as letting the agent make the choice.

6. **Name who owns the misses.** Before you remove a person from a class of actions, write down who is accountable and who pays if the agent is wrong (claims 8, 9 and 12). If no one owns the misses, you have not closed the loop — you have only moved the risk.

7. **Use the scores to improve the next cycle.** Treat a miss as data: refine the goal, the check, or the threshold (claims 3, 5 and 7). A loop with no measured result cannot improve itself. A loop that only chases a thin metric can improve the wrong thing (see the Goodhart counterpoint).

**How to read these sources.** Ev Oputa’s four-way vocabulary (in, on, over, and out of the loop) is his practitioner framing in the Nov 7, 2026 X Article; the widely used public terms are human-in-the-loop, human-on-the-loop, and human-in-command (claim 8). DeepSeek’s AIME movement is a training-run result on verifiable contest problems, not a workplace productivity number. Crosby’s “about 20%” is the firm’s own description of its gray-area work, as reported by the Times. The Daily Gazette link is a syndicated full-text reprint of Kessler’s Nov 7, 2026 *New York Times* article; the figures were checked against that text.

## Open questions
- How should a team write a key result for work that is part precision and part taste (for example a customer note that must be factually right and also sound like the company)?
- How often should an overseer sample work that is “on metric,” so Goodhart gaming and incomplete checks do not hide for a full cycle?
- When the verifier is incomplete — tests that miss a class of bugs, or a key result that does not cover the real objective — who owns the misses the check cannot see?
- What is a light first version of this pattern for a non-technical team this week: one checkable workflow with a written goal, a pass/fail check, a threshold for stepping in, and a named owner?

## Corrections to the pitch (for the writer)
- Frame the argument around closing the loop on work you can check, and using goals plus measured results to oversee by exception. The sources do not show that removing step-by-step approvals raises a success rate; they show that approvals become a bottleneck, and that a reliable check is what lets a loop run and improve.
- The travel story is Airbnb’s Q3 2026 results (newsroom letter of Nov 5, 2026), not a generic trending recap. Use Airbnb’s own guest-behavior figures (claim 10) and cite the 80% “enjoy researching” figure from Skift/McKinsey (claim 11), not from an X summary.
- X recaps blended separate facts into one sentence (“80% enjoy research — viewing dozens of listings, 25+ photos, messaging hosts 90% of the time”). Those are not a single statistic. “Dozen listings” and “25 photos per listing often” appear in third-party earnings-call transcripts, not in Airbnb’s official letter; they were dropped.
- The “who pays when the agent gets it wrong” caution traces to the Crosby insurance story in the Times, not to the recap post. After buying the policy, Crosby removed lawyer review only from gray-area tasks it says are about 20% of its work; lawyers still supervise clearly legal work.
- Do not present Ev Oputa’s in/on/over/out list as standard industry vocabulary; attribute it to him if you use it.
- Do not attribute any specific tool or workflow to Jon. Nothing Claude-centric or single-vendor.
