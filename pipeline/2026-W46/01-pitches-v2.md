# Pitches — week 2026-W46

## 1. Oversee, don't block: close the loop on work you can check
- **Reader:** operators and team leads, technical or not, who spend the day approving each agent step instead of checking results
- **Why now:** this week the argument is no longer whether agents should act, but when the human should step out of the way. Operators are asking how to run agents without approving every step ([Ev Oputa, Nov 7](https://x.com/Evoputa/status/2118923396948140506)), and a live caution is "before you take the human out of the loop, decide who pays when the agent gets it wrong" ([example, Nov 10](https://x.com/Kitfungchai/status/2119945930531348607)). At the same time, a trending story argued that people still want to do taste-heavy tasks like travel booking themselves ([X story, Nov 7](https://x.com/i/trending/2119080226143756746)), while other teams are putting humans on direction and review and letting non-technical staff build with AI ([X story, Nov 6](https://x.com/i/trending/2118849874212884679)). Output figures in that second story need Rhett to confirm against the original source.
- **Promise:** a short test for which work is precision (measurable, checkable) versus taste (judgment, preference), plus a numbered oversight pattern — what to check, when to step in, when to leave the loop alone — that a non-technical person can run this week
- **Rank reason:** this is Jon's own idea, and it is the clearest first-article topic he can speak to as himself without centering a tool he doesn't use. It serves builders and non-technical operators in the same piece, has a natural analogy (oversee vs block), and leaves a named counterpoint: taste work and high-stakes work should stay in the loop, and someone has to own the misses. It follows last week's lesson that numbered checks and analogy hooks help read-through. Scores: useful 5, clear 5, credible 4, concise 4, iterative 4.

## 2. Start with one agent: a test for when a "team" is overkill
- **Reader:** builders and non-technical operators who are spinning up several agents and losing track of the work
- **Why now:** a Nov 9 trending debate set simple, one-thread chats against multi-agent "chief of staff" setups ([X story, Nov 9](https://x.com/i/trending/2119611729978278325)), and readers are already asking how to coordinate agents across tools and projects ([example](https://x.com/rxsupercool/status/2119428984765391337)). The productivity multiples quoted around those setups are anecdotal; Rhett should treat them as unverified.
- **Promise:** a plain, numbered test you can run this week to decide whether one agent in one thread is enough, and when a specialist team is actually worth the overhead
- **Rank reason:** timely, useful today, and tool-agnostic. It is a different question from the unused chief-of-staff pitch in 01-pitches.md (when not to build the team, not how to queue one). It ranks below #1 because the debate still leans on executive anecdotes and is easier to overfit to one person's workflow. Scores: useful 5, clear 4, credible 3, concise 4, iterative 3.

## 3. Don't read every line: a numbered way to check agent work
- **Reader:** managers, analysts, and tech leads whose job is shifting from doing the work to checking what an agent produced
- **Why now:** engineers are publicly rethinking whether they still need to read every line ([X story, Nov 5](https://x.com/i/trending/2118122337409417398)), and a recurring audience question this week is "how do you know the result is actually what you asked for?" ([example](https://x.com/DevTalksRo/status/2117969080615313573)). A Nov 10 story on growth work made the same shift for non-engineers: the scarce skill is evaluating AI output, not producing the first draft ([X story, Nov 10](https://x.com/i/trending/2119970228033712132)). Salary and job-posting figures in that story need Rhett to confirm.
- **Promise:** a short, numbered review checklist (what to sample, what to verify against the original request, what to reject) that a non-technical reviewer can use on any agent output this week
- **Rank reason:** it extends W45's "evaluate an agent before you trust it" into the weekly craft of checking work, and it uses numbered checks, which last week's look-back said drove read-through. It ranks third because it can blur into #1 (oversight) if the draft isn't tight, and the jobs and salary claims around it are noisy to source. Scores: useful 4, clear 4, credible 3, concise 4, iterative 5.
