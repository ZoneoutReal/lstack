# Don't wait for the company rollout: run a capped, four-week opt-in

Whether an employee uses generative AI at work can look like a personal choice. It appears to track closely whether the employer says yes. In a Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis working paper, Bick et al. (2025) report from a nationally representative US survey that "86% of respondents whose employer encourages genAI use report adopting it, compared to just 13% among those whose employer does not" (p. 16). Yet Lin and Parker (2025) of the Pew Research Center found that "half say their employer neither encourages nor discourages the use of AI chatbots at work," while 12% say their employer encourages it (p. 23).

If silence is the norm, and encouragement goes with far more use, what can a cautious leader do while the company-wide rollout is still being planned? The purpose of this article is to propose a sanctioned, capped, four-week opt-in for Grok team bots. Secondly, it answers the "not worth the cost" objection with official list prices, a real spend cap, a stop rule, an honest test of time saved and a fair way to choose who joins.

## Two terms: shadow AI and sanctioned use
Writing in *SN Computer Science*, Puthal et al. (2025) define shadow AI as the "deployment and usage of AI tools, models, or systems by employees of an organization without explicit endorsement, supervision, or management by IT and cyber security departments" (Introduction, para. 1). Here, sanctioned use means AI use the organization has explicitly endorsed and supervises (our wording, built by contrast with Puthal et al., 2025). The US surveys measure encouragement, which is not the same as sanction, and neither measures forbidden or hidden use (Bick et al., 2025; Lin & Parker, 2025). We therefore do not call the 13% shadow AI users; without a clear signal, use is left to chance.

## Silence is the default, and encouragement is the lever
Employer encouragement is the strongest pattern in the US evidence. Bick et al. (2025) found that "a worker is 65 percentage points more likely to use genAI for work if their employer encourages them to do so" (p. 17). Writing for Gallup, Pendell (2025) found that "only 22% say their organization has communicated a clear plan or strategy," and that employees who strongly agree it has are "three times as likely to feel very prepared to work with AI."

The evidence has limits. The authors "caution that the estimated effect of employer encouragement is not necessarily causal" (Bick et al., 2025, p. 17). Their figures come from 2024 survey waves, and Kemp (2026), also writing for Gallup, reports that 52% of US workers now use AI in their role. No study we found compares opt-in with mandated rollouts. The case is therefore narrow: a sanctioned, bounded opt-in may serve a team more than waiting does, and the strongest version asks the exec to sanction it out loud.

## What it costs, and how to cap it
These are US list prices as of Nov 20, 2026, before tax, and "Prices subject to change"; "Third Party model usage in Cursor" costs extra (SpaceX Super Intelligence, n.d.). Grok Bot "is included on Premium, Super, and Ultra. Plus does not include Grok Bot." Team plans are "Premium at $30 per seat, Super at $100 per seat, and Ultra at $200 per seat," and tiers can be mixed (SpaceX Super Intelligence, 2026e). Team Bots "are available on Team and Enterprise plans" (SpaceX Super Intelligence, 2026d), and "Meetings are available on Team, Enterprise, Ultra, and Super plans" (SpaceX Super Intelligence, 2026a). Bot usage "draws from that seat's shared weekly usage pool," and no official page says how much work that covers, so the pilot must measure it (SpaceX Super Intelligence, 2026e).

"Capped spend" has to mean two settings, because "A separate Grok Bot spend cap is not available today" (SpaceX Super Intelligence, 2026c):

1. **A fixed number of seats at list price.** A mid-period joiner gets "a new seat ... charged a prorated amount for the rest of the billing period" (SpaceX Super Intelligence, 2026i).
2. **A monthly additional usage limit**, set before day one. "Alerts do not stop usage. To put a hard stop on paid Additional Usage, set a monthly additional usage limit." Leave Auto Top-up off (SpaceX Super Intelligence, 2026g).

Maximum spend is seat cost per billing period plus the limit per calendar month. Write it down. Stopping takes deliberate steps: a removed member's "seat stays on your plan" until an admin selects Reduce seats, and removed seats "come off at the end of the billing period" (SpaceX Super Intelligence, 2026i). "On Team plans, a canceled seat stays active until the end of the billing period," so cancel "at least 24 hours prior to renewal" (SpaceX Super Intelligence, n.d., 2026b). The boundary is membership: on Teams, "every member has access without any admin action ... There is no switch to turn it off," so run the pilot in its own team workspace (SpaceX Super Intelligence, 2026c, 2026i).

## Write the stop rule before you start
Capped, time-boxed pilots are normal practice. The UK Government Digital Service (GDS) ran a Microsoft 365 Copilot trial "involving 20,000 government employees" over three months (GDS, 2025b). The GOV.UK Service Manual advises weighing "how much it'd cost against how much of an improvement you think you could make," adding: "It's not a failure to stop at the end of the discovery phase if your research shows that's the best thing to do" (GDS, 2021). It notes "around 4 to 8 weeks is typical" (GDS, 2021), so four weeks is the short end. Before day one, get the exec to agree to an end date, the cost-versus-improvement test, kill conditions (e.g., usage below a set level or spend reaching the cap) and an off-limits list.

## Weigh cost against time saved
We found no published hours-saved figure for Grok Bot, so this article claims none. The UK trial reported that "Trial participants saved an average of 26 minutes a day" (GDS, 2025b), but the Department for Science, Innovation and Technology (2025) notes that "Figures are derived from self-reported daily time savings." The UK Department for Business and Trade (DBT) found "Small time savings," yet "did not find evidence that time savings have led to improved productivity" (DBT, 2025). Self-reported savings seem weak evidence, so the pilot should time a few repeat tasks before and during it, and set that against the maximum spend.

## Pick who joins
Volunteers are not a random sample. DBT let colleagues "volunteer for a licence, until around ~70% of licences had been allocated," then assigned the rest to a random sample "stratified by directorate group and grade" (DBT, 2025). In current US work use, "genAI adoption is 6 percentage points higher for men," and the gap shrinks once occupation and employer encouragement are accounted for (Bick et al., 2025, pp. 10, 17). Self-selection may carry such gaps into a pilot, so mix the group: mostly volunteers, plus a few who did not volunteer, with self-led training, which DBT found "more effective than formal departmental training sessions" (DBT, 2025).

## Clear review first, and keep some work off the bot
A central rollout has real strengths. The UK Government AI Playbook asks that "You know how to use AI securely" and "You work with commercial colleagues from the start" (GDS, 2025a), and several Grok Bot controls are "Enterprise only" (SpaceX Super Intelligence, 2026c). The pilot should invite that review, not route around it. Some work should stay off the bot. The UK trial found limits with "complex, nuanced, or data-heavy aspects of work," and "Human oversight was required at all times" (GDS, 2025b). The Playbook asks that "humans validate any high-risk decisions influenced by AI" (GDS, 2025a). Name that work in writing.

## How it runs day to day
lstack, an open template for a small team of content bots, shows a structure, not results: "Files are the handoff," and "A human approves every publish. No exceptions" (lstack, 2026a). Each bot has a checkable goal (e.g., "KR1: 100% of claims sourced"; lstack, 2026b). A pilot can borrow that shape.

## Takeaway
Leaders should be obligated to decide, not to stay silent. The US evidence suggests that encouragement goes with far more use, and that few US workers say their employer gives it (Bick et al., 2025; Lin & Parker, 2025). A cautious exec can sanction one bounded pilot and ask for five things:

1. Sanction, said out loud, for one pilot team workspace.
2. A fixed seat count and tier at list price.
3. A monthly additional usage limit, with Auto Top-up off.
4. An end date four weeks out, with Reduce seats on the calendar.
5. A stop rule, a time-saved measure beyond self-report and an off-limits list, agreed before day one.

Security and commercial review come first. If the pilot does not pay off, stopping is the result, and the organization will have saved time and money finding out.
