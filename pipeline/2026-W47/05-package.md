## Article
### Title
Don't wait for the company rollout: run a capped, four-week opt-in

### Body
Whether an employee uses generative AI at work can look like a personal choice. It appears to track closely whether the employer says yes. In a nationally representative US survey, "86% of respondents whose employer encourages genAI use report adopting it, compared to just 13% among those whose employer does not" (Bick et al., 2025, p. 16). Yet many employers have taken no side. Pew found that "half say their employer neither encourages nor discourages the use of AI chatbots at work," while 12% say their employer encourages it (Lin & Parker, 2025, p. 23).

If silence is the norm, and encouragement goes with far more use, what can a cautious leader do while the company-wide rollout is still being planned? The purpose of this piece is to propose a sanctioned, capped, four-week opt-in for Grok team bots. Secondly, it answers the "not worth the cost" objection with official list prices, a spend cap built from settings the product actually has, a stop rule written before day one, an honest way to weigh cost against time saved, and a fair way to choose who joins. The following sections define two terms, make the case, and then lay out the plan.

## Two terms: shadow AI and sanctioned use
Two terms carry this argument, so we define them first. Puthal et al. (2025) define shadow AI as the "deployment and usage of AI tools, models, or systems by employees of an organization without explicit endorsement, supervision, or management by IT and cyber security departments" (Introduction, para. 1). No source we found defines sanctioned use on its own. In this piece, sanctioned use means AI use the organization has explicitly endorsed and supervises; the wording is ours, built by contrast with Puthal et al. (2025). Both terms describe permission and oversight, not how much people use a tool.

One caution follows. The US survey data measure encouragement, which is not the same as sanction (Bick et al., 2025; Lin & Parker, 2025). An employer can allow a tool without encouraging it, and neither study measures use the employer has forbidden or does not know about. We therefore do not claim that the 13% are shadow AI users. We can suggest only that, without a clear signal from leadership, use is low and left to chance.

## Silence is the default, and encouragement is the lever
Employer encouragement is the strongest pattern in the US evidence. Bick et al. (2025) found that "a worker is 65 percentage points more likely to use genAI for work if their employer encourages them to do so" (p. 17). In a later survey, the US had the lowest share of workers receiving none of three supports (encouragement, training and tools), yet that share was still 44%, against 69% in France and 70% in Italy (Bick et al., 2026, p. 28). Gallup found that 40% of US workers had used AI at work a few times a year or more, while "only 22% say their organization has communicated a clear plan or strategy" (Pendell, 2025). It seems that many organizations are leaving this lever unused.

The evidence has limits. The authors "caution that the estimated effect of employer encouragement is not necessarily causal" (Bick et al., 2025, p. 17). Their 86% and 13% come from 2024 survey waves, and US use has risen since: Gallup reports that 52% of US workers now use AI in their role (Kemp, 2026). No newer public split by encouragement was found. Nor does any study we found compare opt-in with mandated rollouts head to head. The case here is therefore narrow: a sanctioned, encouraged, bounded opt-in may serve a team better than waiting, not better than a mandate.

Clear direction from the top appears to help as well. When employees strongly agree that leadership has communicated a clear plan for AI, they are "three times as likely to feel very prepared to work with AI and 2.6 times as likely to feel comfortable using AI in their role" (Pendell, 2025). Peers and managers may matter too. In a UK government pilot, "Users were also anecdotally less likely to use M365 Copilot if their colleagues and line managers were hesitant about its use" (Department for Business and Trade [DBT], 2025). For this reason, the pilot should not go around leadership. The strongest version asks the exec to sanction it and say so out loud.

## What it costs, in official list prices
These are US list prices on the vendor's pages as of Nov 20, 2026, before tax. The pricing page says "Prices subject to change," so re-check before you sign (SpaceX Super Intelligence, n.d.).

- Grok Bot "is included on Premium, Super, and Ultra. Plus does not include Grok Bot" (SpaceX Super Intelligence, 2026d).
- Team plans: "Premium at $30 per seat, Super at $100 per seat, and Ultra at $200 per seat. Each seat includes the same usage as the matching individual tier." You can mix tiers (e.g., "three Premium seats and one Super seat"). "Plus isn't available for teams" (SpaceX Super Intelligence, 2026d).
- Team Bots, shared bots that everyone on a team can work with, "are available on Team and Enterprise plans. Meetings are available on Team, Enterprise, and Ultra plans" (SpaceX Super Intelligence, 2026c).
- Enterprise is sold through sales, with no list price. The pricing page points organizations that "need invoicing, pooled usage, or advanced security" to Enterprise (SpaceX Super Intelligence, n.d.).
- The monthly prices exclude "Third Party model usage in Cursor" (the coding tool in the same subscription), which "must be purchased separately" (SpaceX Super Intelligence, n.d.).

One gap remains. No official page states how much work a seat's usage covers in hours, tasks or dollars. Bot usage "draws from that seat's shared weekly usage pool, not a separate additive pool," and on self-serve team plans "Seats do not share weekly pools" (SpaceX Super Intelligence, 2026d, 2026g). The pilot will have to measure this.

## Make the cap real: two settings, one boundary
"Capped spend" has to mean two settings, because there is no cap just for the bot: "A separate Grok Bot spend cap is not available today. Account-level on-demand controls apply" (SpaceX Super Intelligence, 2026b).

1. **A fixed number of seats at list price.** Seat cost for the pilot is the number of pilot seats times the tier price, for each billing period the pilot touches (SpaceX Super Intelligence, 2026d). Someone who joins mid-period gets "a new seat ... charged a prorated amount for the rest of the billing period" (SpaceX Super Intelligence, 2026f).
2. **A monthly additional usage limit.** This "controls how much paid Additional Usage you can use in a calendar month." Additional Usage is paid usage beyond each seat's included weekly pool, plus the third-party model usage above. Set it before day one. "Alerts do not stop usage. To put a hard stop on paid Additional Usage, set a monthly additional usage limit." Auto Top-up is "off unless you turn it on"; leave it off or bounded (SpaceX Super Intelligence, 2026e, 2026g).

Your maximum pilot spend is the seat cost for each billing period, plus the additional usage limit for each calendar month the pilot touches. Write that number down before you start.

Stopping takes deliberate steps. A removed member "lose[s] access to the team right away," but "Their seat stays on your plan" until an admin selects Reduce seats, and "Removed seats come off at the end of the billing period" (SpaceX Super Intelligence, 2026f). "On Team plans, a canceled seat stays active until the end of the billing period," and the pricing page says to cancel "at least 24 hours prior to renewal to avoid additional charges" (SpaceX Super Intelligence, n.d., 2026a). Put the Reduce seats step on the calendar before the end date.

The boundary is team membership. "On the Teams plan, Grok Bot is enabled by default, and every member has access without any admin action ... There is no switch to turn it off" (SpaceX Super Intelligence, 2026b). "People can belong to more than one workspace," so run the pilot in its own team workspace and add only the people in it (SpaceX Super Intelligence, 2026f). If the seat count and the limit are not set up front, the cost case falls apart.

## Write the stop rule before you start
Time-boxed, capped pilots are normal practice, even in large organizations; the examples here are from the UK government. It ran a cross-government Microsoft 365 Copilot trial "from 30 September 2024 to 31 December 2024, involving 20,000 government employees," after earlier pilots "limited to 300 licences at most" (Government Digital Service [GDS], 2025b). The UK DBT ran "One thousand licences ... for a 3-month pilot" (DBT, 2025).

Stopping is a legitimate result if the test is decided in advance. The UK Service Manual advises weighing "how much it'd cost against how much of an improvement you think you could make," and adds: "It's not a failure to stop at the end of the discovery phase if your research shows that's the best thing to do. In fact, you'll be saving time and money" (GDS, 2021). It also notes that "around 4 to 8 weeks is typical" for a discovery (GDS, 2021), and the UK Copilot pilots ran three months, so four weeks is the short end. We suggest stating that openly. Write these down, and get the exec to agree before day one:

- The end date, four weeks out.
- The cost-versus-improvement test you will apply at the end.
- Kill conditions (e.g., usage below a level you name, spend reaching the cap, or a quality incident you define).
- The off-limits list (see below).

## Weigh cost against time saved without fooling yourself
We found no published hours-saved figure for Grok Bot, so this piece does not claim one. The UK cross-government trial reported that "Trial participants saved an average of 26 minutes a day," but the press release notes that "Figures are derived from self-reported daily time savings" (Department for Science, Innovation and Technology, 2025; GDS, 2025b). The DBT evaluation found "Small time savings were observed across most use cases," yet "The evaluation did not find evidence that time savings have led to improved productivity," and users "completed Excel data analysis more slowly and to a worse quality and accuracy than non-users" (DBT, 2025). It seems that self-reported time savings are weak evidence of value, so an exec who distrusts "hours saved" has a point.

The pilot should produce its own evidence instead. We suggest deciding in advance how time saved will be measured beyond self-report (e.g., timing a few repeat tasks before and during the pilot, or comparing samples of work from inside and outside it). That result can then be set against the maximum spend written down on day one.

## Pick who joins: volunteers plus a few who did not raise a hand
Everyone should get a chance to raise a hand, but volunteers are not a random sample. DBT let colleagues "volunteer for a licence, until around ~70% of licences had been allocated," then assigned the remaining ~30% to a random sample "stratified by directorate group and grade to improve representation" (DBT, 2025). In current US use of generative AI for work, "genAI adoption is 6 percentage points higher for men" (Bick et al., 2025, p. 10), and in the authors' statistical model the gap shrinks once occupation and employer encouragement are taken into account (Bick et al., 2025, p. 17, Table 1). We can suggest that pure self-selection may carry such gaps into a pilot, while a deliberate, encouraged pilot may narrow them.

Training and firsthand experience matter as well. DBT found "self-led training [was] more effective than formal departmental training sessions" (DBT, 2025). Gallup found that 68% of US employees with firsthand experience using AI with customers said it had a positive effect, while only 13% of those without it believed it would (Pendell, 2025). For this reason, mix the group: mostly volunteers, plus a few people who did not volunteer, with hands-on, self-led training.

## Clear review first, and keep some work off the bot
A central rollout does some things better. Security, network controls, identity, data handling and procurement are easier to get right once than many times. The UK Government AI Playbook asks that "You know how to use AI securely" and "You work with commercial colleagues from the start" (GDS, 2025a). Grok Bot's own rollout checklist asks admins to review proxy and TLS inspection, sign-in method and inherited policies before rollout, and several controls are "Enterprise only" (e.g., Network Controls, Team Secrets and Auto-review rules; SpaceX Super Intelligence, 2026b). The pilot should therefore invite security and commercial review, not route around it.

Some work should stay off the bot. The UK trial observed limitations "when dealing with complex, nuanced, or data-heavy aspects of work," and "Human oversight was required at all times" (GDS, 2025b). DBT reported hallucinations, and slower, less accurate Excel work (DBT, 2025). The Playbook asks for "meaningful human control at the right stage," including "ensuring that humans validate any high-risk decisions influenced by AI" (GDS, 2025a). Name the off-limits work in writing: high-risk decisions, and nuanced or data-heavy tasks without a human review.

## How it runs day to day: a structural example
lstack, an open template for a small team of content bots, is one example of the structure, not of results. Its working agreements state: "Files are the handoff." "A human approves every publish. No exceptions." "Work goes back to the previous step at most once" (lstack, 2026a). Each bot has a role file with its own OKRs (e.g., the researcher's "KR1: 100% of claims sourced"; lstack, 2026b). A pilot can borrow the shape: a human approves anything that goes out, work hands off in a place everyone can see, and each bot has a goal someone can check.

## Takeaway
Leaders should be obligated to decide, not to stay silent. The US evidence suggests that encouragement goes with far more use, and that few US workers say their employer gives it (Bick et al., 2025; Lin & Parker, 2025). A cautious exec does not need to make a company-wide decision to act on that. They can sanction one bounded pilot and ask for five things:

1. Sanction, said out loud, for one pilot team workspace.
2. A fixed seat count and tier at list price.
3. A monthly additional usage limit, with Auto Top-up off.
4. An end date four weeks out, with Reduce seats on the calendar before it.
5. A stop rule, a way to measure time saved beyond self-report, and an off-limits list, agreed before day one.

Security and commercial review should come first. If the pilot does not pay off, stopping is the result, and the organization will have saved time and money finding out.
