## Article
### Title
Don't wait for the company rollout: run a capped, four-week opt-in

### Body
Same text as `04-final.md` at b146b79, unchanged, sources list included.

Your people are not waiting for the rollout. In Denmark, "even among workers whose employers neither encourage chatbot use, provide an enterprise chatbot, nor offer training, about 41% have used AI chatbots at work at least once, and 7% use them daily" [13]. Microsoft and LinkedIn's 2024 survey of 31,000 people in 31 countries found "78% of AI users are bringing their own AI tools to work" [13]. In the US, Gallup found 40% have used AI at work a few times a year or more, while "only 22% say their organization has communicated a clear plan or strategy" [13].

So the choice in front of a cautious exec is not "bots or no bots." It is sanctioned use or unsanctioned use. This paper is a one-page plan for the sanctioned version: a small team opts in to Grok team bots for four weeks, with official list prices, a spend cap built from settings the product actually has, a stop rule written before day one, an honest way to weigh cost against time saved, and a fair way to choose who joins.

## Why a sanctioned opt-in beats waiting
The case here is narrow. It is not that opt-in beats a mandate. No study we found compares the two head to head. The case is that people already use these tools without waiting, and that employer backing goes with more use and better reported results.

In the same Danish study, "Encouraged use, Enterprise chatbots, and Training each raise extensive-margin take-up rates to 76%, 57%, and 69%, respectively" (the share of workers who have used chatbots at work at least once). With all three in place, "93% of workers ... report having used AI chatbots at work, and 28% use them daily." Encouraged-use policies are "consistently associated with users reporting greater time savings, improved quality, and enhanced creativity" [14]. These are associations, and "encouraged" means allowed and promoted, not required.

Leadership matters too. When employees strongly agree that leadership has communicated a clear plan for AI, Gallup found they are "three times as likely to feel very prepared to work with AI and 2.6 times as likely to feel comfortable using AI in their role" [16]. Peers and managers matter as well: in the UK Department for Business and Trade (DBT) pilot, "Users were also anecdotally less likely to use M365 Copilot if their colleagues and line managers were hesitant about its use" [15].

That is why this pilot should not go around leadership. The strongest version asks the exec to sanction it and say so out loud.

## What it costs, in official list prices
These are US list prices on the vendor's pages as of Nov 16, 2026, before tax. The pricing page says "Prices subject to change," so re-check before you sign [1].

- Grok Bot "is included on Premium, Super, and Ultra. Plus does not include Grok Bot" [2].
- Team plans: "Premium at $30 per seat, Super at $100 per seat, and Ultra at $200 per seat. Each seat includes the same usage as the matching individual tier." You can mix tiers, for example "three Premium seats and one Super seat." "Plus isn't available for teams" [3].
- Team Bots, shared bots that everyone on a team can work with, "are available on Team and Enterprise plans. Meetings are available on Team, Enterprise, and Ultra plans" [4].
- Enterprise is sold through sales, with no list price. The pricing page points organizations that "need invoicing, pooled usage, or advanced security" to Enterprise [3].
- The monthly prices exclude "Third Party model usage in Cursor" (the coding tool in the same subscription), which "must be purchased separately" [1].

One honest gap: no official page states how much work a seat's usage covers in hours, tasks or dollars. Bot usage "draws from that seat's shared weekly usage pool, not a separate additive pool," and on self-serve team plans "Seats do not share weekly pools" [2][3]. The pilot will have to measure this.

## Make the cap real: two settings, one boundary
"Capped spend" has to mean two settings, because there is no cap just for the bot: "A separate Grok Bot spend cap is not available today. Account-level on-demand controls apply" [5].

1. **A fixed number of seats at list price.** Seat cost for the pilot is the number of pilot seats times the tier price, for each billing period the pilot touches [3]. Someone who joins mid-period gets "a new seat ... charged a prorated amount for the rest of the billing period" [6].
2. **A monthly additional usage limit.** This "controls how much paid Additional Usage you can use in a calendar month." Additional Usage is paid usage beyond each seat's included weekly pool, plus the third-party model usage above [2]. Set it before day one. "Alerts do not stop usage. To put a hard stop on paid Additional Usage, set a monthly additional usage limit." Auto Top-up is "off unless you turn it on"; leave it off or bounded [5].

Your maximum pilot spend is the seat cost for each billing period, plus the additional usage limit for each calendar month the pilot touches. Write that number down before you start.

Stopping takes deliberate steps. A removed member "lose[s] access to the team right away," but "Their seat stays on your plan" until an admin selects Reduce seats, and "Removed seats come off at the end of the billing period" [6]. "On Team plans, a canceled seat stays active until the end of the billing period," and the pricing page says to cancel "at least 24 hours prior to renewal to avoid additional charges" [6]. Put the Reduce seats step on the calendar before the end date.

The boundary is team membership. "On the Teams plan, Grok Bot is enabled by default, and every member has access without any admin action ... There is no switch to turn it off" [7]. "People can belong to more than one workspace," so run the pilot in its own team workspace and add only the people in it [7].

If the seat count and the limit are not set up front, the cost case falls apart. That is the condition the whole proposal rests on.

## Write the stop rule before you start
Time-boxed, capped pilots are normal practice, even for large organizations. The UK government ran a cross-government Microsoft 365 Copilot trial "from 30 September 2024 to 31 December 2024, involving 20,000 government employees," and earlier pilots had been "limited to 300 licences at most." DBT ran "One thousand licences ... for a 3-month pilot" [8].

Stopping is a legitimate result if you decide the test in advance. The GOV.UK Service Manual says to weigh "how much it'd cost against how much of an improvement you think you could make," and that "It's not a failure to stop at the end of the discovery phase if your research shows that's the best thing to do. In fact, you'll be saving time and money" [9].

Write these down and get the exec to agree before day one:
- The end date, four weeks out. GOV.UK says "around 4 to 8 weeks is typical" for a discovery [9], and the UK Copilot pilots ran three months [8], so four weeks is the short end. Say so.
- The cost-versus-improvement test you will apply at the end [9].
- Kill conditions: usage below a level you name, spend reaching the cap, or a quality incident you define.
- The off-limits list (see below).

## Weigh cost against time saved without fooling yourself
There is no published hours-saved figure for Grok Bot, so this paper does not claim one. The pilot has to produce its own.

Be careful how. In the UK cross-government trial, "Trial participants saved an average of 26 minutes a day," but GOV.UK notes that "Figures are derived from self-reported daily time savings" [10]. DBT found "Small time savings were observed across most use cases," yet "The evaluation did not find evidence that time savings have led to improved productivity." Users "completed Excel data analysis more slowly and to a worse quality and accuracy than non-users" [11]. In Denmark, researchers estimate "precise null effects on earnings and recorded hours," ruling out effects larger than 2% two years after ChatGPT launched [18].

An exec who distrusts "hours saved" has a point. So decide in advance how you will measure it beyond self-report: time a few repeat tasks before and during the pilot, or compare samples of work from inside and outside the pilot. Then set that against the maximum spend you wrote down.

## Pick who joins: volunteers plus a few who didn't raise a hand
Early adopters are scattered across a company, so everyone should get a chance to raise a hand. But volunteers are not a random sample. DBT let colleagues "volunteer for a licence, until around ~70% of licences had been allocated," then assigned the remaining ~30% to a random sample "stratified by directorate group and grade to improve representation" [12].

Pure opt-in can also widen gaps. In a study of 18,000 Danish workers, "Women are 16 percentage points less likely to have used the tool for work," and the gap was still 12 points among coworkers in the same workplace with similar tasks. Workers "are often hindered by employer restrictions and a perceived need for training" [17].

So mix the group: mostly volunteers, plus a few people who did not volunteer. Make training self-led and hands-on. DBT found "self-led training [was] more effective than formal departmental training sessions," and Gallup found 68% of employees with firsthand experience using AI with customers said it had a positive effect, while only 13% of those without it believed it would [15].

## Clear review first, and keep some work off the bot
A central rollout does some things better. Security, network controls, identity, data handling and procurement are easier to get right once than many times. The UK Government AI Playbook asks that "You know how to use AI securely" and "You work with commercial colleagues from the start" [19]. Grok Bot's own rollout checklist asks admins to review proxy and TLS inspection, sign-in method and inherited policies before rollout [19]. Several controls are "Enterprise only," including Network Controls, Team Secrets and Auto-review rules [7].

So invite that review instead of routing around it. If your company requires security or commercial sign-off before any self-serve team plan, get it before day one.

Some work should stay off the bot. The UK trial observed limitations "when dealing with complex, nuanced, or data-heavy aspects of work," and "Human oversight was required at all times" [C1]. DBT reported hallucinations, and slower, less accurate Excel work [11]. The Playbook asks for "meaningful human control at the right stage," including "ensuring that humans validate any high-risk decisions influenced by AI" [19]. Name the off-limits work in writing: high-risk decisions, and nuanced or data-heavy tasks without a human review.

## How it runs day to day: a structural example
A pilot runs better with a few plain rules. lstack, an open template for a small team of content bots, is one example of the structure, not of results. Its working agreements say: "Files are the handoff." "A human approves every publish. No exceptions." "Work goes back to the previous step at most once." Each bot has a role file with its own OKRs, for example the researcher's "KR1: 100% of claims sourced" [20]. Borrow the shape: a human approves anything that goes out, work hands off in a place everyone can see, and each bot has a goal you can check.

## Takeaway
Don't ask the exec for a company-wide decision. Ask for five things:

1. Sanction, said out loud, for one pilot team workspace.
2. A fixed seat count and tier at list price.
3. A monthly additional usage limit, with Auto Top-up off.
4. An end date four weeks out, with Reduce seats on the calendar before it.
5. A stop rule, a way to measure time saved beyond self-report, and an off-limits list, agreed before day one.

Clear security and commercial review first. If the pilot doesn't pay off, stopping is the result, and you will have saved time and money finding out.
