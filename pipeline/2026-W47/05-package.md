## Article
### Title
Don't wait for the company rollout: sanction a capped, four-week opt-in pilot of AI agents

### Body
Whether an employee uses AI at work can look like a personal choice, yet it appears to track whether the manager says yes. Gallup (n.d.) reports that employees in AI-adopting organizations who strongly agree that their manager "actively supports" their team's use of AI are "1.7x as likely to use AI a few times a week or more" (the page does not date this figure). Yet few organizations have spelled out their intent: "As of August 2026, 26% of U.S. employees say their organization has communicated a clear plan for integrating AI into current practices" (Gallup, n.d.). Only about a quarter of US employees, then, say their organization has communicated a clear AI plan, which measures a plan rather than encouragement.

If few organizations have a clear plan, and support goes with more use, what can a cautious leader do while the company-wide rollout is still being planned? The purpose of this article is to make the case for sanctioning a capped, four-week opt-in pilot of AI agents across several uses. Secondly, it shows that results differ by use, why tasks should be timed rather than estimated, what the evidence does not yet cover, and how to write a stop rule in plain words before day one.

## Three terms
Writing in *SN Computer Science*, Puthal et al. (2025) define shadow AI as the "deployment and usage of AI tools, models, or systems by employees of an organization without explicit endorsement, supervision, or management by IT and cyber security departments" (Introduction, para. 1). Here, sanctioned use means AI use the organization has explicitly endorsed and supervises (our wording, built by contrast with Puthal et al., 2025). An AI agent is software built on a generative AI model that can plan and carry out steps toward a goal, using tools to act in other systems (for example, processing a return rather than only answering a question about it), sometimes with little human oversight (Center for AI Standards and Innovation [CAISI], 2026; U.S. Government Accountability Office [GAO], 2025). GAO (2025) adds that "There is no universally agreed upon definition of an AI agent" (p. 1). The Gallup surveys cited below measure manager support and whether a clear AI plan has been communicated. Neither is the same as sanction, and neither measures hidden use (Gallup, n.d.).

## Support goes with use
"As of May 2026, nearly four in 10 employees (36%) in AI-integrating organizations strongly agree that their manager supports their team's use of AI" (Gallup, n.d.). The 36% is for organizations integrating AI and managers who "support" team AI use; the 1.7x above is for organizations adopting AI and managers who "actively support" it. This is a survey link, not proof that manager support causes use (our wording), and no study we found compares opt-in with mandated rollouts. A sanctioned opt-in may therefore serve a team more than waiting does, if the exec sanctions it out loud.

## Real pilots already cover several uses
A pilot need not bet on one task. In a randomized trial across 66 firms, Dillon et al. (in press) gave workers a tool "integrated into applications they already used at work for email, meetings, and writing" (abstract). *Label: data from 2023 to 2024.* Everyday use is similarly mixed. Writing for Gallup, Kemp (2026) reports that "Among AI users, the most common uses of AI are writing and editing (51%), search or research (49%), and general assistance or problem-solving (39%)." *Label: US survey fielded May 6 to 20, 2026.*

The closest US example of a pilot is the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania (2025), where "The Commonwealth equipped 175 employees to use ChatGPT Enterprise" (p. 8) from "14 state government agencies" (p. 9), and "These employees volunteered to explore this new technology" (p. 2). Their uses were spread out: "Writing assistance, text generation, drafting emails 36%," "Research, idea exploration, learning new topics 27%" and "Summarizing 13%" (p. 16). "Users estimated saving 95 minutes per day with ChatGPT" (p. 3), but that figure rests on "Estimates reported by participants in exit survey" (p. 33), and "ChatGPT reduced, but did not eliminate, human input into final work products" (p. 34). *Label: US state government; data from 2024 to 2025; one tool; self-reported time; "OpenAI led trainings" (p. 11).*

## Results differ by use
The more carefully measured studies suggest that results depend on the task and on who uses the tool. Each tested one tool.

- **Email fell; meetings did not.** Dillon et al. (in press) found that "In the second half of the 6-month experiment, the 80% of treated workers who used the tool spent two fewer hours on email each week" (abstract), yet "We find no similar shifts in the time workers spent in Teams meetings" (p. 8). *Label: peer-reviewed, forthcoming in American Economic Review: Insights; data from 2023 to 2024; mostly Microsoft Research authors, testing Microsoft 365 Copilot.*
- **Drafting got a little faster, and quality gains differed by seniority.** In a trial with "133 practicing patent lawyers at eleven U.S. intellectual property law firms," Autor et al. (2026) found that "Treated lawyers spent about 10 fewer minutes on the 10-day drafting task than controls (p = 0.05), whose mean was 110 minutes" (p. 2). While lawyers had AI, quality rose, "with larger gains among junior lawyers." On a later task done without AI, the advantage "was concentrated entirely among senior lawyers," and "Junior lawyers showed no average gain" (abstract). *Label: US randomized trial, not yet peer-reviewed; data from May 2025 to February 2026; funded by Google, with Google staff among the authors.*
- **Note-taking strain eased.** Writing in *JAMA Network Open*, Olson et al. (2025) report that after 30 days with an AI note-taker, "burnout among those working in ambulatory clinics decreased significantly from 51.9% to 38.8%" (Key Points). *Label: data from 2024; clinicians' visit notes, not office meetings; volunteers, before-and-after survey; "There was no control group"; authors tied to the vendor.*
- **Agents shortened chats but lowered ratings.** In customer service, Wang et al. (2026) report that "AI deployment reduces average chat duration and has limited effects on retrial rates, but substantially lowers ratings for AI-eligible chats" (abstract). *Label: not US (Taobao, China); preprint; data from August 2024; one author at Alibaba Group.*

Across US workers, average gains are modest. The FRED Blog of the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis (2026) reports that "the share of work hours saved thanks to AI assistance grew from 1.6% to 2.2%." *Label: self-reported; data from Q3 2024 to Q2 2026.* For this reason, a pilot should judge each use on its own, not the pilot as a whole.

## Time the work rather than ask people
Felt speed and clock speed can differ. In "a preregistered large-scale behavioral study (N = 1237)," Yu et al. (2026) found that "While actual completion times between independent completion and AI-assisted completion did not differ, participants predicted AI to be significantly faster," and that "participants reported lower subjective effort with AI despite equivalent completion times" (abstract). *Label: preprint; simple lab tasks, not real jobs; published May 2026, collection dates not stated.* The FRED figures carry the same warning: "The information is based on how respondents self-report their 'time saved,' so the measurements are inherently approximate" (Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, 2026). A pilot should therefore time real tasks by the clock.

## What the evidence does not show, and where it is thin
- **Saved time may not show up in output.** Dillon et al. (in press) "do not detect shifts in the quantity or composition of workers' tasks" (abstract). *Label: data from 2023 to 2024.*
- **Delegating more can mean more errors.** Ma et al. (2026) found that "participants who delegated more completed more of the task, but were also more likely to produce invalid solutions," while "Experts more often resisted this tradeoff, sacrificing completion to decompose the task and scrutinize agent outputs" (abstract). *Label: preprint; 20 professionals in a think-aloud study; mostly Microsoft authors; collection dates not stated.*
- **Speed can cost quality.** The agents in Wang et al. (2026) shortened chats, but customers rated those chats lower (abstract).
- **Help is uneven and can fade without AI.** Junior patent lawyers kept no edge on the later task done without AI (Autor et al., 2026).

Some gaps should be said plainly. We found one randomized field trial of agents acting on their own at work, outside the US and in customer service only (Wang et al., 2026, data from 2024). We found none of an opt-in pilot, and none that ran several tools. Pennsylvania lists teams as an open question: "How do best practices and adoption change when teams, not just individuals, add GenAI to their workflows?" (Commonwealth of Pennsylvania, 2025, p. 54). Much of the field data above was also collected before 2026. The case for a pilot rests on these gaps as much as on the gains: a short, measured test is how a team finds out.

## Design the pilot
Before day one, list the use cases, name a tool and an owner for each, and record a baseline: time and errors on 3 to 5 real tasks per use case, done the usual way. This structure is our judgement. Set a spending limit in each tool before day one, which is also our judgement.

Volunteers are not a random sample. In Pennsylvania, "Pilot participants were not a representative sample of Commonwealth employees" (Commonwealth of Pennsylvania, 2025, p. 12). We suggest mostly volunteers, plus a few who did not volunteer.

A central rollout has real strengths in security and purchasing, so the pilot should clear review first. An agent that acts on its own can also act wrongly, so we suggest starting each one read-only, with a named reviewer and an off-limits list in writing. These are our judgements, not findings.

## The stop rule, in plain words
Deciding the test up front makes stopping a normal outcome. The GOV.UK Service Manual advises that "It's not a failure to stop at the end of the discovery phase if your research shows that's the best thing to do," and notes that, for the discovery phase of a new service, "around 4 to 8 weeks is typical" (Government Digital Service [GDS], 2021). We suggest judging each use case on five measures, against the baseline, at week 2 and again at week 4.

| Measure | What to count | Bar (ours) |
|---|---|---|
| **Time by the clock** | Clock time on the same real tasks | Not faster by week 4: drop the use |
| **Errors and rework** | Mistakes found; work sent back | Higher than baseline: drop, even if faster |
| **Minutes checking AI output** | Reviewer time spent checking or fixing | Checking eats the time saved: drop |
| **Use falling off** | Share who used it last week | Most stop by week 2, no recovery by week 4: drop; team sets the cut-off |
| **Security incidents** | Sensitive data shared, actions out of scope, access problems | Any one: pause that tool the same day and review |

Sources: the measures draw on Dillon et al. (in press), Autor et al. (2026), Yu et al. (2026), Ma et al. (2026), Wang et al. (2026), the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis (2026) and the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania (2025); see References. Every bar and the stop rule are ours; no source sets a threshold.

For customer-facing uses, we suggest also watching customer ratings, since that is where the agents in Wang et al. (2026) fell short. Use can also fall off. Pennsylvania participants "predicted that they would use ChatGPT for 6 tasks on average but went on to report half as many use cases" (Commonwealth of Pennsylvania, 2025, p. 43), and some were "reluctant to use ChatGPT due to uncertainty about how it processes and stores input data" (p. 48). *Label: data from 2024 to 2025.*

At week 4, we suggest that any use case meeting every bar be proposed for a longer, wider pilot, with its numbers. Use cases that miss a bar end, and access for them is removed. If none pass, the pilot ends. Write this down before day one.

## Takeaway
Leaders should be obligated to decide, not to stay silent. Manager support goes with more use, and the evidence suggests real but uneven help that differs by task, and only one randomized field trial of agents acting on their own, in customer service outside the US, with data from 2024 (Wang et al., 2026). A cautious exec can sanction one capped, four-week opt-in pilot and ask for five things:

1. Sanction, said out loud.
2. A short list of use cases, each with a tool, an owner and a baseline.
3. A spending limit in each tool.
4. A named reviewer, an off-limits list and read-only access first.
5. The five-measure stop rule, checked at week 2 and week 4, agreed before day one.
